Abstract
Traditional agent-based urban mobility simulations often rely on rigid rule-
based systems that struggle to capture the complexity, adaptability, and
behavioral diversity inherent in human travel decision making. Inspired by
recent advancements in large language models and AI agent technologies,
we introduce GATSim, a novel framework that leverages these advance-
ments to simulate urban mobility using generative agents with dedicated
cognitive structures. GATSim agents are characterized by diverse socioeco-
nomic profiles, individual lifestyles, and evolving preferences shaped through
psychologically informed memory systems and lifelong learning. The main
contributions of this work are: 1) a comprehensive architecture that inte-
grates urban mobility foundation model with agent cognitive systems and
transport simulation environment; 2) a hierarchical memory designed for
efficient retrieval of contextually relevant information, incorporating spatial
andtemporalassociations; 3)planningandreactivemechanismsformodeling
adaptive mobility behaviors which integrate a multi-scale reflection process
to transform specific travel experiences into generalized behavioral insights.
Experiments indicate that generative agents perform competitively with hu-
man annotators in role-playing scenarios, while naturally producing realistic
macroscopic traffic patterns. The code for the prototype implementation is
publicly available at https://github.com/qiliuchn/gatsim.
Keywords: Agent-based simulation, Urban mobility, Generative agents

1. Introduction
Agent-based modeling (ABM) is a powerful tool for simulating complex
systems, including urban traffic, epidemic spread, and market dynamics
(Macal, 2016). In the domain of urban planning and traffic management,
urban mobility simulation systems play a crucial role by enabling researchers
and policymakers to model complex travel patterns and evaluate infrastruc-
ture changes (Bastarianto et al., 2023). While existing systems like MATSim
(MATSim Community, 2025) have demonstrated significant capabilities in
modeling large-scale urban mobility flows, they face fundamental limitations
in capturing the heterogeneity, complexity and adaptability of human travel
behavior (Huang et al., 2022).
Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) offer a transformative
opportunity to address these limitations. The pioneering work Park et al.
(2023) introduced “generative agents” to demonstrate the feasibility of using
LLMs to create more realistic simulation agents. However, existing genera-
tive agent studies fall short in addressing the complexities of urban mobility
decision-making. Their general-purpose cognitive architectures are not well-
suited to producing realistic travel behaviors, as they typically lack adaptive
learning mechanisms essential for simulating human-like adaptability in ur-
ban mobility scenarios.
Given these limitations, there is a clear need for a more specialized ap-
proach to model the dynamics of urban mobility. To this end, we introduce
GATSim (Generative-Agent Transport Simulation), a novel framework de-
signed to leverage the strengths of LLMs while addressing the challenges
of urban mobility modeling. In contrast to previous studies on generative
agents, we customize the memory, planning, reaction and reflection mecha-
nisms specifically for better modeling urban activities. GATSim agents pos-
sess diverse socioeconomic attributes, individual lifestyles, and evolving pref-
erences that shape their mobility decisions through psychologically-informed
memory systems and lifelong learning mechanisms. This paper makes the
following contributions to the field of urban mobility simulation:
1. A comprehensive framework for generative-agent-based urban
mobility simulation: We propose GATSim, a novel framework that
integrates urban mobility foundation model with agent cognitive struc-
tures and transport simulation environment.

2. Related Work
Transportation simulation methodologies span a spectrum from macro-
scopic approaches that model aggregate traffic flows to microscopic frame-
worksthatcaptureindividualmovementsandbehaviors. Agent-basedmodel-
ingrepresentsaparticularlypowerfulmicroscopicapproachwhereautonomous
entities make independent decisions based on local information and behav-
ioral rules. This section first reviews recent applications of LLMs in trans-
portation and urban systems; then reviews the evolution of transportation
simulation studies, beginning with traditional macroscopic systems, then ex-
amining recent advances in LLM-based agent simulation across various do-
mains,andconcludingwithananalysisofcurrentresearchgapsthatmotivate
our work.
2.1. LLMs in Transportation and Urban Systems
The transportation domain has seen growing interest in LLM applica-
tions, spanning prediction, simulation, and control tasks. Traffic predic-
tion systems like UrbanGPT (Li et al., 2024d) and OpenCity (Li et al.,
2024c) employ spatiotemporal attention mechanisms for city-scale forecast-
ing, while specialized models like TransGPT (Wang et al., 2024d) and Traf-
ficGPT(Zhangetal.,2024a)focusontransportationcontroltasks. Forurban
6

simulation and behavioral modeling, several approaches have emerged. Xu
et al. (2023) proposed Urban Generative Intelligence (UGI), incorporating
city-specific foundation models for various urban tasks. Jin et al. (2023)
combined LLM agents with the CARLA simulator for microscopic driving
behavior, while Yuan et al. (2023) developed frameworks based on psycho-
logical theories like Maslow’s hierarchy of needs.
Recent work has specifically targeted mobility behavior generation. Shao
et al. (2024) introduced Chain-of-Planned-Behaviour (CoPB) for structured
mobilitygeneration, whileWangetal.(2024e)developeddesire-drivenframe-
works for activity trajectory synthesis. Li et al. (2024b) proposed Desire-
driven Autonomous Agents (D2A) for travel diary generation, and Wang
et al. (2024b) introduced self-consistency approaches to align LLMs with
real-world activity data. Bhandari et al. (2024) focused on synthetic travel
survey generation using LLM-based systems. Several studies have explored
mobility prediction using historical data. Research by Wang et al. (2023a);
Gong et al. (2024); Chen et al. (2025) focuses on leveraging location-based
service (LBS) data and mobility histories to predict future travel patterns,
demonstratingthepotentialofLLMsforunderstandingspatial-temporalmo-
bility dynamics. These diverse applications highlight the growing recognition
of LLMs as powerful tools for modeling complex urban and transportation
systems.
2.2. Traditional Urban Mobility Simulation Methods
Urban mobility simulation methodologies have evolved from macroscopic
to increasingly microscopic approaches over several decades. The Four-Step
Model (Ortúzar and Willumsen, 2002) represents one of the earliest macro-
scopic approaches. Travel-chain-based modeling emerged in the 1980s and
1990s (Kitamura, 1996), explicitly modeling sequences of trips individuals
make throughout a day rather than treating trips in isolation. Activity-based
modeling became particularly influential starting in the mid-1990s (Bowman,
1995; Axhausen and Gärling, 1992), expanding the travel-chain concept by
focusing on individual activities, their scheduling, duration, location, and
sequence. Landmark implementations including the Day Activity Schedule
model(BowmanandBen-Akiva,2001)andArentzeandTimmermans(2004).
More recent studies such as Galland et al. (2014), Marilleau et al. (2006),
Bucchiarone (2019), and Li et al. (2021) adopt more advanced expert models
and explore a wider range of scenarios, including emergency excavation, car-
pooling, and autonomous vehicle simulation. Agent-based simulation gained
7

widespread adoption in urban traffic modeling from the late 1990s onwards,
driven by advances in computational power and influential frameworks such
as TRANSIMS (Smith et al., 1995) and MATSim (Horni et al., 2016). Con-
temporary platforms including AIMSUN (Casas et al., 2010) and CUBE
(Wong, 2008) represent mature implementations of these methodologies.
Traditionalagent-basedmodelingapproachesprimarilyrelyonrule-based
systems, optimization methods, or reinforcement learning. Rule-based ap-
proaches remain prevalent due to their interpretability, while optimization-
based methods enable agents to maximize utility or reward functions (Chary-
par and Nagel, 2005; Horni et al., 2016). Recent innovations have extended
these approaches through deep learning integration (Wong et al., 2018) and
activity pattern recognition from trajectory data (Adetiloye and Awasthi,
2019; Lou and Cheng, 2020). However, traditional methods fundamentally
limit the realism of human behavior modeling due to their inability to cap-
ture behavioral diversity, their reliance on rigid and non-adaptive decision-
making, poor generalization across contexts, and the absence of mechanisms
for social interaction and learning.
2.3. LLM-Based Agent Simulation and Platforms
Recent advances in LLMs have enabled the development of generative
agents—autonomousentitiesthatproducehuman-likebehaviorsthroughnat-
ural language reasoning and memory-based learning. The seminal work by
Park et al. (2023) established the foundational architecture. In economics
and finance, agents support macroeconomic simulation (Li et al., 2023; Hor-
ton, 2023) and financial market modeling (Gao et al., 2024b). Social science
applications include opinion polling (Park et al., 2024), crisis management
(Xiao et al., 2023), and information diffusion analysis (Zhang et al., 2024b;
Rossetti et al., 2024). Game theory research explores competitive and co-
operative agent behaviors (Xie et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024; Fontana et al.,
2024). These successes across varied contexts demonstrate the broad appli-
cability of LLM-based agent simulation.
Large-scale multi-agent platforms have emerged to support complex sim-
ulations at scale. AgentScope (Gao et al., 2024a) and OASIS (Yang et al.,
2024) focus simulations with thousands to millions of interacting agents,
demonstrating technical feasibility of large-scale deployment. These plat-
forms potentially can provide robust computational infrastructure for agent
coordination and parallel execution.

2.4. Research Gaps and Opportunities
Despite significant progress, existing approaches face critical limitations
that prevent comprehensive urban mobility simulation:
Fragmented Focus: Current studies predominantly address isolated
aspects such as next location prediction, lacking integrated frameworks that
combine realistic population synthesis, behavioral modeling, and system dy-
namics.
Limited Behavioral Complexity: Existing systems fail to capture the
fullspectrumofhumandecision-makingprocesses,includingspatial-temporal
memory utilization, adaptive learning from experience.
Inadequate Social Dynamics: Most approaches treat agents as inde-
pendententities, missingthecollaborativecoordination, informationsharing,
and social influence that characterize real urban mobility systems.
Recent advances in generative agents (Park et al., 2023) and large-scale
multi-agent systems (Gao et al., 2024a; Yang et al., 2024) offer new opportu-
nities to bridge these gaps. In this work, we present a comprehensive frame-
work that integrates advanced agent cognition with a scalable simulation
architecture, enabling realistic behavioral diversity while ensuring computa-
tional efficiency for practical applications.
3. Simulation Environment
The simulation core manages the fundamental computational processes
including transportation network management, population management and
simulation loop execution. The key distinguishing feature of the proposed
simulation core is its capability to generate individualized perceptions for
agents and execute the plan-action-reflection cognitive loop that enables be-
havioral learning and adaptation.
In the prototype, the simulation core is designated as backend module.
The backend serves as the simulation entry point, loading environment con-
figuration from gatsim/map, creating the generative agent population, and
executing the main simulation loop. By organizing these processes into a
modular structure, the backend ensures that the simulation can be easily
adapted for various urban mobility scenarios.
3.1. Transportation Network Management
The simulation core employs a hierarchical transportation network rep-
resentation strategy utilizing three complementary formats: graph, tilemap,
9

and bitmap representations. Graph-structured data serves as the primary
input for LLM-based scenario generation and reasoning. The transportation
network is formally described by a graph G = (V,E) where V represents the
set of vertices (nodes) and E represents the set of edges (links). Activity
locations such as apartments, offices, and schools are designated as “facili-
ties”, which exclusively serve as trip origins and destinations. Each facility
is spatially associated with a specific node in the transportation network.
Transit networks are modeled following the approach in Spiess and Florian
(1989), representing transit lines as sequences of transit links that connect
to the road network through artificial boarding and alighting links. This
representation enables seamless multimodal routing while maintaining com-
putational efficiency. For LLMs with image processing capabilities, GATSim
supports bitmap input to enable visual reasoning about network topology
and spatial relationships. Tilemap representations facilitate map editing op-
erations and support real-time web interface animations, while bitmap-based
visualizations provide the primary user interaction medium, as illustrated in
Figure 3.
Traffic dynamics are modeled using a point-queue system (a simplified
traffic flow model where vehicles are treated as discrete entities that form
queues at nodes and links, with instantaneous movement between queue
positions rather than continuous spatial dynamics), where each network ele-
ment maintains queues to govern agent movement. Link and facility capacity
constraints are incorporated to simulate realistic congestion effects. Future
versions will incorporate more sophisticated traffic flow models to enhance
realism.
3.2. Population Management
At the start of the simulation, the simulation core either creates or loads
the population, which consists of agent objects described in later sections.
Each agent’s activity state and location are updated continuously during the
simulation loop. Agents’ travel experiences are generated throughout the
simulation and inserted into their respective memory modules. The simula-
tion core may also provide constraint-checking functionalities. For instance,
the simulation core enforces the requirement that each agent must return
home by the end of the day. If this condition is not met, the agent is tele-
ported home, and a reflection process is triggered, prompting the agent to
consider why they failed to complete their journey as expected.

The user interface (Figure 1 (b)) of simulation environment serves four

5. Agents
ThegenerativeagentsrepresentthecoreinnovationoftheGATSimframe-
work, embodying autonomous entities with cognitive architectures that en-

5.1. Agent Memory
The memory system represents a critical component of generative agents,
enablinglearningfromexperience,preferenceformation,andadaptivedecision-
making over time. The GATSim framework transcends simple information
storage to create a cognitive architecture that mirrors human memory pro-
cesseswhileaddressingtheuniquerequirementsofurbanmobilitysimulation.
In the following sections, we first discuss memory structure and organization,
then present memory retrieval and forgetting mechanisms.
Memory Structure and Organization. The GATSim agent memory employs
a hierarchical structure inspired by cognitive psychology, comprising short-
term and long-term memory components with distinct but interconnected
functions. Short-term memory serves as the agent’s working cognitive space,
maintaining dynamically updated information for the current simulation day
13

including activity plans, ongoing states, travel paths, and immediate percep-
tions. Long-term memory stores accumulated information across extended
time horizons, forming the foundation for behavioral learning and adapta-
tion.
To unify representation of diverse experience types—perceived events, so-
cial conversations, and internal reflections—we employ a ConceptNode class
structure where each node encapsulates comprehensive attributes including
textual content, semantic embeddings, extracted keywords, spatial cover-
age, temporal scope, importance scores, creation timestamps, and access
histories. In contrast to previous implementations maintaining separate spa-
tial memory structures (Park et al., 2023), our unified approach integrates
spatial-temporal information directly into long-term memory. This archi-
tectural decision enables cross-modal reasoning for adaptive behavior. For
instance, an agent reflecting on metro delays can automatically connect con-
gestion patterns with conversations about service disruptions or previously
discovered alternative routes during similar situations. The unified structure
simplifies retrieval mechanisms by enabling comprehensive memory search
without requiring separate processing pipelines for different memory types.

temporal nature of urban mobility decisions. GATSim agent memory em-

strategies: keyword matching, semantic similarity, and spatial-temporal rel-

5.2. Agent Activity Planning, Reaction and Reflection
The activity planning and reactive behavior represent core capabilities
that distinguish generative agents from traditional rule-based approaches.
This section presents the cognitive architecture underlying these capabili-
ties, beginning with the activity planning process that synthesizes contex-
tual information and social coordination to generate daily schedules. We
then examine the dynamic plan revision mechanisms that enable real-time
adaptation to changing environment. Afterwards, we discuss the multi-scale
16

reflection mechanism that transforms specific travel experiences into general-
ized behavioral insights, enabling continuous learning and behavioral evolu-
tion over extended simulation periods. Finally, we present refinements that
enhance the framework’s robustness and performance.
Activity Planning Process. The activity planning process operates through
a cognitive architecture that integrates multiple information sources across
three stages (illustrated in Figure 4).
Contextual Inputs Preparation Stage. Each generative agent ini-
tiates daily planning by synthesizing contextual inputs from three primary
sources: (1) long-term reflections that encapsulate key personal experiences
spanning work-related tasks, social interactions, and family affairs while in-
corporatinganticipationsofupcomingevents; (2)dailyreflectionsfromrecent
experiences that provide immediate behavioral context; and (3) perceived
broadcast events representing shared external occurrences such as museum
exhibitions, weather alerts, or infrastructure disruptions.
Social Coordination Stage. The planning process incorporates social
coordination through collaborative decision-making where agents may initi-
ate conversations with relevant parties before finalizing plans. For instance,
household members might discuss school pickup responsibilities or friends
might coordinate post-work activities. Conversation summaries are added to

Proposed Remedies. To address the identified limitations, we propose a com-
prehensive set of practical strategies that can be implemented within current
technological constraints:
• Leveraging Advanced Base Models: Employing LLMs with en-
hanced reasoning capabilities represents the most direct and conve-
nient approach to improving generative agent performance. We exper-
imented with different versions of Qwen models and found that agents
using the qwen_max generate more reasonable activity plans and ex-
hibit significantly better coordinated behavior compared to the smaller
qwen_plus model, albeit at the cost of increased latency. Concurrent
API calls and batched processing can effectively mitigate latency con-
cerns while preserving quality improvements.

• Iterative Activity Plan Validation: Implementing systematic plan
review processes enables agents to self-correct errors through multiple
validation rounds. For example, an agent initially generated a plan
to use transit for the entire day except for one midday activity; af-
ter multiple checks, the agent successfully identified and corrected this
inconsistency. Generative agents may occasionally forget to include
the “return home” activity at the end of their plan, but they typically
remedy this oversight before day’s end. Since agents naturally adapt
plans throughout the day, perfect initial planning is unnecessary —
continuous refinement proves more effective and realistic.
• Task Decomposition: Generative agents using smaller LLMs (such
as 7B parameter models) struggle to simultaneously generate reflec-
tions and daily activity plans with consistent vehicle usage and task
coordination in a single step. When practical constraints necessitate
using smaller LLMs, we recommend decomposing the task into smaller
components, such as reflection, activity facility decision, travel mode
decision, and departure time decision etc. Even for agents with larger
LLMs, task decomposition remains beneficial, as demonstrated by the
success of Chain-of-Thought reasoning (Wei et al., 2022).
• Prompt Optimization: Long contextual inputs can overwhelm agent
attentionmechanisms,degradinginstructionadherencedespiteexpanded
context windows in modern LLMs. Prompt engineering techniques can
improve generative agent performance, such as placing important and
frequently “forgotten” instructions at the end of prompts and incorpo-
rating redundancy for critical instructions.
• Fine-tuning for Behavioral Alignment: Hallucination presents a
dual challenge — enabling creative behavioral diversity while main-
taining behavior grounded in actual experiences. Fine-tuning offers a
promising approach to reducing unwanted hallucination while simulta-
neously enhancing behavioral diversity.
• Rule-based Validation: Implementing automated validation for crit-
ical domain constraints (e.g. vehicle usage consistency) provides im-
mediate error detection without requiring model modifications. When
violations occur, agents receive specific feedback prompting plan regen-
eration. This lightweight approach offers immediate practical benefits

with minimal implementation overhead.
• Appropriate Abstraction Level Selection: Effective simulation
requires strategic decisions about modeling granularity based on re-
search objectives and computational resources. Key considerations in-
clude: vehicle sharing mechanisms within households, agent perception
radius and frequency, social network complexity and evolution, and
temporal resolution for activity scheduling. These choices fundamen-
tally shape both computational requirements and behavioral realism,
requiring careful calibration for specific application domains.

6.1. Experiment 1: Microscopic Behavior Validation
This section evaluates the realism of individual agent decision-making by
comparing generative agent behaviors with human responses in controlled
mobility scenarios. The evaluation focuses on two key capabilities: reactive
decision-making in dynamic conditions and behavioral learning through ex-
perience accumulation. We conducted comparison between generative agents
and human annotators using role-playing. Five human participants were re-
cruited as annotators, each presented with 10 mobility scenarios carefully
selected from the simulation for a total of 50 evaluation cases. Each scenario
included comprehensive contextual information: simulation environment de-
scription, agent role profile, real-time traffic conditions, environmental per-
ceptions, retrieved memory content. Both generative agents and human an-
notators were asked to provide two outputs for each scenario: a reflection
capturing their reasoning process and an action plan for immediate next
steps. To ensure unbiased evaluation, we employed GPT-o1 as an indepen-
dent judge (Zheng et al., 2023), randomly permuting the order of AI and
human responses to eliminate positional bias effects. The LLM judge out-
puts which response is more realistic, or a “tie”. The evaluation of “being
26

realistic” encompasses multiple dimensions, including contextual appropri-
ateness (alignment with the given scenario, agent profile, and environmental
context), reasoning coherence (logical consistency and justification of the de-
cision process), behavioral plausibility, and human-like priorities (Liu et al.,
2025).
Note that we are comparing generative agents with human annotators
under role-playing tasks rather than comparing with humans with their ac-
tual profiles and observed real-world behaviors. This choice reflects practical
constraints in transportation research. Recruiting a large and diverse pop-
ulations to participate in extended mobility simulations using their actual
profiles is challenging and raises privacy concerns. Role-playing by trained
annotators provides a controlled, replicable benchmark that captures human
reasoning patterns while maintaining experimental validity.

6.1.2. Comparative Performance Results
Of the 50 scenarios evaluated, generative agents were judged more realis-
tic in 23 cases (46.0%), human annotators prevailed in 20 cases (40.0%), and
7 cases (14.0%) resulted in ties. The slight advantage for generative agents
can be attributed to their superior ability to process and synthesize exten-
sive contextual information. Human annotators frequently struggled with
the volume of information presented, including detailed activity histories,
event logs, social interaction records, and environmental conditions. This re-

human cognition. While LLMs can maintain attention across extensive tex-

with stronger spatial reasoning and common-sense grounding. We emphasize
that our claim is not that LLM judge are “better” than humans in absolute
terms, but rather that they demonstrate competitive behavioral realism in
role-playing scenarios while offering advantages in information processing ca-
pacity and consistency.

tested whether generative agents significantly outperform human annotators.

7. Conclusion
This work presents GATSim, a novel framework that introduces gener-
ative agents to transportation simulation, contributing to the fundamental
paradigm shift from rule-based to intelligence-based modeling. The frame-
workenablesagentstocontinuouslylearnandadaptthroughdedicatedmem-
orysystemsandreflectionmechanisms,creatingrealisticbehavioralevolution
over time. GATSim demonstrates the potential to mimic emergent proper-
34

ties of human societies, revealing underlying mechanisms of urban mobility
that are difficult to capture through conventional modeling approaches. The
primary contributions of this study are the comprehensive GATSim frame-
work and the specialized agent cognitive architectures encompassing mem-
ory, planning, reaction, andreflectionmechanismstailoredforurbanmobility
simulation.
The microscopic validation reveals that generative agents perform com-
petitively with human annotators in role-playing scenarios. The macroscopic
validation demonstrates realistic traffic evolution over multiple simulation
days, with agents adaptively adjusting departure times, routes, and mode
choices to achieve system states comparable to traditional user equilibrium
models.
The GATSim framework opens numerous avenues for practical applica-
tions in transportation planning and policy analysis. It can enable planners
to easily specify scenarios and analyze outcomes without extensive program-
ming expertise. The behavioral richness of generative agents supports de-
tailed analysis of policy interventions, infrastructure changes, and service
disruptions with greater realism than traditional approaches. The frame-
work’s ability to model social coordination and information sharing makes it
particularly valuable for studying ride-sharing, transit coordination, and cri-
sis response scenarios. Furthermore, the adaptive learning capabilities enable
longitudinal studies of behavioral change in response to new transportation
services, pricing policies, or urban development patterns. The framework’s
modular design facilitates integration with existing transportation models
and data sources, supporting practical deployment in real-world planning
contexts.
Limitations. Despite promising results, several limitations constrain current
framework capabilities. LLM agents demonstrate excessive cooperativeness
compared to human behavior, reflecting their training as helpful assistants
rather than realistic individual personas, potentially overestimating cooper-
ation levels in transportation systems. Multi-modal urban environment data
processing remains challenging, as LLMs still struggle with complex spatial
reasoning despite our framework’s spatial query mechanisms. Memory re-
trieval optimization and consistent behavior maintenance over extended sim-
ulation periods present ongoing challenges. The current traffic flow model
employs a simplified point-queue approach without intersection signal con-
trol, turn movement modeling, or realistic capacity representations. Vali-
35

dation benchmarks for LLM-driven agent-based simulation remain an open
problem, with current methods relying on role-playing scenarios rather than
directreal-worldbehaviorcomparisons. Finally, computationalintensitylim-
its practical deployment to small populations, though advances in model
efficiency may address these constraints over time.
Future Directions. Several promising research directions emerge from this
work. Generic LLM reasoning may not capture transportation-specific de-
cision heuristics that real travelers employ, such as “always add 10 minutes
to Google Maps estimates”. Incorporating these domain-specific heuristics
requires careful prompt engineering or specialized training on transportation
behavior datasets. The current implementation employs static, hand-crafted
social structures including family relationships, friendship networks, and oc-
cupational roles that remain fixed throughout simulation. While generative
agents in principle could model dynamic social structure evolution — al-
lowing relationships to form, strengthen, or dissolve based on agent interac-
tions — this extension would require substantially longer simulation horizons
and introduces complexity beyond transportation-focused validation. Future
work could explore and evaluate this extension for studies specifically exam-
ining the interplay between social dynamics and mobility patterns. Defining
“realistic” behavior is fundamentally challenging and inherently subjective.
WeadoptedtheLLM-as-a-judgeapproachforitsconsistency, reproducibility,
and scalability. Future research could pursue alternative validation frame-
works, such as evaluating the degree to which generated behaviors remain
“in-domain” by comparing their distributional properties with observed real-
world travel data, or developing hybrid approaches that combine automated
judgment with domain expert evaluation. Advances in model compression,
specialized hardware, and LLM inference efficiency will be crucial for prac-
tical deployment at city scale. Finally, comprehensive validation requires
three-way comparisons among real-world observations, traditional simula-
tion baselines, and generative agent framework results at realistic population
scales.

Appendix C. Psychologically-Informed Forgetting Mechanisms
Realistic memory decay modeling prevents unbounded memory growth
while reflecting psychological principles governing human memory retention.
Each memory entry receives an expiration attribute determining its lifes-
pan based on non-linear relationships with importance scores, reflecting the
well-established principle that salient experiences persist longer than mun-
dane ones (Anderson and Schooler, 1991; Wixted and Ebbesen, 1991). The
importance score criteria is shared at gatsim/technical_highlights (Liu
et al., 2025). The lifespan calculation employs power functions aligning with
empirical findings on human memory retention:
lifespan = lifespan +(lifespan −lifespan )×importancepower (C.1)
init min max min
In the prototype, parameters are tuned to each memory type’s distinct
characteristics. Event memories use minimum lifespans of 2 hours and max-
imum of 4 days with power 2.4, allowing transient irrelevant events to fade
quickly while enabling important ones to persist for pattern learning. Con-
versational memories employ 4-hour minimums and 2-day maximums with
power 3.2, reflecting that most coordination content are relevant but only
pertains to immediate needs, while highly important exchanges still warrant
extended retention. Reflective insights use 8-hour minimums and 8-day max-
imums with power 1.6, enabling learned knowledge to guide behavior over
longer timescales (see Figure C.11. This approach mirrors power-law forget-
ting curves observed in psychological studies (Wixted and Ebbesen, 1991)
where retention probability follows P(t) ∝ t−β. Memory retrieval during
reasoning or reflection extends expiration by the initial lifespan, implement-
ing the spacing effect effect (the psychological phenomenon where repeated
exposure to information over time strengthens memory retention) observed
in human memory (Cepeda et al., 2006).
