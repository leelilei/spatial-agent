# Space in Social Simulation

This section synthesizes how space is currently used inside LLM-agent social-simulation systems. Its purpose is not to restate the evidence map row by row, but to convert the coded distribution into a readable account of current practice. The safest headline is that current systems do use space, but they mostly use it through local, semantic, or interaction-level structure rather than through agent-facing configurational abstraction.

## 5.1 From Classical ABM to LLM Agent Societies

The opening subsection should position current LLM-agent social simulation as a partial continuation rather than a clean replacement of earlier social simulation traditions. Classical ABM and adjacent simulation traditions already treated environment structure, movement constraints, and encounter opportunities as behaviorally relevant. LLM-agent systems inherit that broad intuition, but they operationalize it unevenly. In many cases they adopt a socially rich scenario frame without exposing comparably rich spatial structure at the agent interface.

This subsection should therefore set up the main contrast of the chapter. The old simulation question was often how environment structure shapes interaction opportunities. The current LLM-agent question is often how language-driven agents interact inside an environment that is spatially present but only partially formalized at the point of action. That difference helps explain why the current literature is able to stage social worlds without yet strongly demonstrating configurational social effects.

## 5.2 How Space Is Currently Used

The current widened-Core corpus contains `34` coded rows. Behavioral-scale coverage is evenly split between `interaction` and `emergent_social_structure`, with `13` rows each, plus `8` `mixed` rows. Yet the representation side remains concentrated in `L3`, with only one admitted `L4` row and a limited `L5` slice. Read together, these distributions suggest that the literature is much more mature in staging social activity in space than in exposing globally structured space to agents.

The main synthesis of this subsection should be organized around recurring representation patterns rather than around individual systems. The current systems mostly use:

- place labels and named zones;
- semantic scene descriptions and role-play context;
- local co-presence, adjacency, and nearby communication;
- graph or feed structures that organize who sees whom and where interaction can occur;
- occasional embodied or geometry-bearing interfaces;
- very little agent-facing global abstract or configurational structure.

This pattern should be stated as a substantive finding of the review. Space is not absent from current social simulation. Rather, it is typically present as local scene structure, social proximity, or movement context. What remains largely missing is explicit agent-facing structure that would allow the literature to test stronger propositions about configuration-level mediation.

## 5.3 What the Current Evidence Actually Supports

The next subsection should separate presence-of-space claims from effect-of-space claims. The coded corpus contains `19` `observed_effect` rows and `15` `designed_affordance_only` rows, so the literature is not limited to design rhetoric alone. At the same time, those observed-effect rows are heterogeneous and unevenly distributed across representation levels. They support limited claims about observed associations in particular systems, not strong claims that spatial configuration has already been shown to shape LLM-agent social behavior across the field.

The behavioral-scale distribution helps clarify this. `Emergent_social_structure` rows are numerous, but they are overwhelmingly concentrated in `L3`, with just one `L4` case and one `L5` case. `Interaction` rows are where the bridge-layer `L2` recovery mostly appears. `Mixed` rows contribute important embodied and multimodal cases, but they still do not by themselves demonstrate global configurational mediation. The result is a literature that can support claims about how space currently enters systems and about where limited associations have been reported, but not yet claims about a settled mechanism linking configuration to social emergence.

Safe synthesis sentences for this subsection:

- current systems often use space as a local interaction scaffold rather than as a global structural input;
- observed spatial-behavior associations exist, but they remain heterogeneous and mostly below the configurational layer;
- widened bridge cases broaden the representational landscape without erasing the strict-gap reading.

Do not write here:

- that current evidence proves spatial configuration shapes LLM-agent societies;
- that the presence of emergent-social-structure rows implies configuration-level mediation;
- that bridge evidence can be read as field-wide validation.

## 5.4 Space Syntax Propositions as Transferable Hypotheses

This is the subsection where the survey should reconnect to Space Syntax, but only under strict claim discipline. The role of Space Syntax here is not to supply direct evidence about LLM-agent systems. Its role is to provide transferable propositions about how global structure may influence encounter, movement, visibility, co-presence, and downstream social organization in environments where agents can actually access that structure.

Table 6 should be the central artifact. Each row should convert a Space Syntax-style proposition into a testable LLM-agent hypothesis while keeping the evidential boundary explicit. For example, propositions about configurational accessibility, encounter opportunity, or route structure should be framed as hypotheses that future systems could test if they expose suitable agent-facing spatial input. They should not be framed as if current LLM-agent systems have already confirmed them.

The safest rhetorical pattern is:

- physical-space evidence suggests a transferable proposition;
- current LLM-agent systems rarely expose the corresponding representation layer directly;
- future work could test whether the proposition survives under controlled agent-facing input.

This subsection should end by reinforcing the central diagnosis of the chapter: current social-simulation systems already contain enough spatial machinery to motivate a serious spatial agenda, but not enough configurationally explicit evidence to close the question.
